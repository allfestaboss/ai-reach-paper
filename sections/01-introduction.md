# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The question is asked constantly and measured rarely

Whether large language models can carry out professional work is discussed
continuously and evidenced thinly. Surveying the Japanese-language commentary on
one such profession — tax practice — we found a large volume of writing
asserting that these systems cannot be trusted with professional judgement, and,
as supporting evidence, two kinds of artefact: scores on a general accountancy
examination, and vendors' own accuracy claims for document recognition. Neither
measures whether the work can be done.

Part of the difficulty is that professional work is not question answering. A
liquefaction assessment is a computation carried out on measured borehole data
under a published national standard, producing a number that determines
foundation type and remediation cost. A quantity takeoff is a set of measurements
governed by a national surveying standard, in which the deduction rules are the
substance. A civil drawing is a file that a conforming reader must accept.
Grading these requires that someone possess the correct answer, and obtaining
the correct answer is the expensive part.

The tempting shortcut is to author the answer oneself. We consider this
disqualifying: a reference solution written by the same person who designed the
task cannot be checked by that person, and the resulting benchmark measures
agreement with its author.

## 1.2 Four kinds of existing benchmark

Existing work on evaluating these systems in professional or applied settings
falls into four groups.

**Examination-style question answering.** MMLU and its Japanese counterparts —
JMMLU, JMED-LLM, IgakuQA119 — pose questions drawn from professional
examinations. Ground truth is unambiguous because the examination supplies it.
The subject selects among options; nothing is produced that a practitioner would
recognise as work output.

**Single-domain task suites.** ATLAS and related work evaluate tariff
classification against published customs rulings; BIM-Edit evaluates
natural-language editing of IFC building models; AECV-Bench evaluates
multimodal understanding of architectural and engineering drawings. These
address real professional artefacts and take ground truth from an external
authority. Each is confined to one domain, so an effect measured within it
cannot be compared against a domain where it might behave differently.

**Reliability under repetition.** τ-bench introduced `pass^k`, the probability
that all of k repeated runs succeed, and reported the gap it exposes between
average and worst-case behaviour; subsequent work has extended reliability
measurement to long-horizon and production-like conditions. This axis is
orthogonal to accuracy and is now standard practice. Success is treated as
binary, which is appropriate for task completion and, as we show in §3.2.3,
insufficient where the answer is a set.

**Multi-metric holistic evaluation.** HELM measures seven metrics — accuracy,
calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency — for every
scenario, so that cost and latency are not lost behind a headline score. Its
scenarios are language tasks rather than professional deliverables.

Across all four groups, we are not aware of any that reports how many defects
were found in its own reference solutions, or by what means. This is the gap
this paper addresses.

## 1.3 Building eight benchmarks surfaced a problem in benchmark construction

We set out to measure how far these systems reach into Japanese professional
practice, and built eight benchmarks — 33 tasks across civil and architectural
CAD, quantity surveying, geotechnical assessment, mechanical tolerancing,
building information modelling, customs classification and tax law — to a single
design. That design requires the inputs, the procedure and the reference
solutions to come from national standards, published specifications and
administrative rulings rather than from us (§2.1), and requires the grader to
reject deliberately corrupted submissions before any score is reported (§2.2).

Running them produced a result about the benchmarks rather than about the
subjects. The reference solutions contained errors, and the machinery built to
validate the benchmarks did not find them. **The solvers did.** Of fifteen
defects found on the examination side of one benchmark, fourteen were located by
the systems being tested; our calibration and adversarial suites found one.

This is not accidental. The grader compares a submission to the reference; the
calibration recomputes the reference along its own specified procedure; the
adversarial cases are generated by corrupting the reference. All three are
functions of the reference solution, and none can register an error in it. In
the sharpest case, a reference solution integrated a geotechnical index below
the bottom of the borehole, placing 60 percent of one reported value in depth
that was never drilled — and passed the grader, passed calibration against hand
computation to a difference of zero, and passed twelve of twelve adversarial
cases. A solver noticed because it held the borehole record and the borehole
record ends.

## 1.4 Contributions

1. **An argument that a benchmark cannot validate its own reference solution**,
   because the checks conventionally used for that purpose are defined in terms
   of it, together with a case in which a defect passed all three (§3.1.1–3.1.2).

2. **Evidence on where such defects are actually found.** Fifteen
   examination-side defects in one benchmark, fourteen of them located by the
   solvers; four more in a second benchmark, all by solvers (§3.1.3). We argue
   that this distribution follows from the structure rather than from chance
   (§3.1.4).

3. **Two practices that follow**: solver reports about the task should be
   collected and adjudicated rather than discarded, and defect counts should be
   published, since a benchmark reporting none is indistinguishable from one
   that did not look (§3.1.4).

4. **Cross-domain measurement of three effects already documented in the
   literature**, holding the design fixed: supplying the governing material
   ranges from moving a task 25 → 97 to changing none of 61 answers under a
   5.4-fold increase in material; scores saturate while cost separates by 2.6 to
   4.9 times; the fraction of answers that change under identical repetition
   ranges from 0 to 82 percent across domains. Within the last, we identify a
   pattern that success-rate reliability metrics cannot express — half of the
   unstable cases score identically across five runs by five different routes
   (§3.2).

5. **Eight benchmarks released in full** — tasks, reference solutions,
   adversarial suites, run records and defect logs — under permissive terms with
   persistent identifiers (§7). Given that the author of the reference solutions
   is also the author of the defect count, independent inspection is the only
   remedy available, and we have tried to make it cheap.

## 1.5 What this paper does not claim

It does not rank models: the arms differ in the material and tooling supplied,
not in the underlying model (§4.3.1). It does not claim the three effects in
§3.2 as discoveries; each is documented, and what is contributed is the spread
across domains under one design. And it does not solve the problem it
identifies — §4.1.1 sets out why the detector that worked here is opportunistic,
why no bound on residual defects follows from it, and why every number in §3.2
should be read as conditional on reference solutions of unknown remaining error.
